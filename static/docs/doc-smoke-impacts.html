Note on Statistics: Here we focus on the impacts of smoke on PM2.5 and O3 concentrations

PM2.5 due to smoke

To identify the presence of smoke in a region, we use the NOAA Hazard Mapping System (HMS) fire and smoke product (Ruminski et al., 2011; Kaulfus et al., 2017; Buysse et al., 2019). It is based on multiple geostationary satellites and gives a smoke density as high, medium or low. However, as the HMS product is derived from satellite data, it does not provide any vertical information on smoke plumes. Our previous analysis (Buysse et al., 2019) indicates that enhanced PM2.5 at the surface is found on 30-80% of days with an overhead smoke plume, depending on the location. Nevertheless, the HMS smoke product is critical to identify days when regional PM2.5 concentrations are possibly influenced by smoke (Kaulfus et al., 2017). We define any location that is within the boundaries of an HMS smoke plume as HMS=1 and a site with no overhead smoke as HMS=0.

We combine the HMS product with surface PM2.5 to identify the presence of smoke at the surface. But this leaves the question of what PM2.5 value or criteria should we use to define smoke influence? Figure 1 shows the distribution of PM2.5 on HMS=0 and HMS=1 days.

While the means of these two distributions are significantly different and the HMS=1 data show a greater occurrence of high PM2.5 days, the distributions have substantial overlap. Our goal is to choose a PM2.5-criteria that is most likely associated with smoke and is unlikely to be due to local pollution alone. We note that this is different from "outlier" detection in statistics. Since our goal is to identify any significant contribution to the PM2.5 concentrations, down to a few μg m-3, using an outlier method would mean that most moderate smoke days would not be classified as having a smoke influence.

Previously, we used the mean plus one standard deviation (Mean + 1 SD) of a sites' HMS=0 PM2.5 concentrations as a criteria to define smoke influence for the GAM v1 dataset (Lee and Jaffe, 2023; 2024). But the figure above shows that the PM2.5 distributions are not normally distributed and so the standard deviation is not a good metric to use for these distributions. Instead, we use a method that is more suited for non-normal distributions called the median absolute deviation (MAD, Leys et al., 2013). The MAD is defined as:

MAD = M2 |X(i) – M1|

Where X(i) is the distribution of PM2.5 observations, M1 is the median of the PM2.5 data and M2 is the median of the absolute deviations from M1. Since PM2.5 data have a strong seasonality (highest in winter in the absence of wildfire smoke), the MAD values are calculated for each site and each month separately. This is why it's necessary to have multiple years of data to identify enough HMS=0 days. Typically, we use a minimum of 4-6 years of data.

We define the PM2.5-criteria as the Median + ½ MAD and Median + 1 MAD, which is approximately the 67th and 80th percentiles of the HMS=0 distribution at each site. Each dataset we generate includes both PM2.5 criteria used at each site (e.g., median+1 MAD and median + ½ MAD). The average PM2.5-criteria (Median + 1 MAD), averaged for all months range from 2.6 μg m-3, at very clean sites along the west coast, to 17.9 μg m-3 at more polluted urban locations. So, this points out the greater challenge of identifying moderate smoke levels at more polluted urban locations. At the same time, we note that the mean and median PM2.5-criteria are 8.51 and 8.46 μg m-3, respectively, so this is not an issue at the vast majority of sites and more than 93% of the 803 O3 sites (i.e., GAM v2) we consider have PM2.5-criteria less than 12 μg m-3.

Note that the PM2.5 criteria can differ between datasets due to variations in calculation methodologies and time periods. As mentioned above, the criteria for the GAM v1 dataset (May–September 2018–2023) were based on the Mean + 1 SD method and calculated as a single fixed value for each site. In contrast, the GAM v2 dataset (April–October 2019–2024) utilizes the Median + 1 MAD method calculated on a monthly basis, yielding seven distinct values (one for each month from April to October) per site. These variations in both statistical metrics and temporal resolution may lead to differences in criteria between the two versions, primarily affecting days with PM2.5 concentrations near the threshold.

Finally, one variable to pay attention to in our datasets is the "Quant_PM2.5" (Quantile PM2.5). This value represents the percentile of each daily PM2.5 observation based on the Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function (ECDF) of PM2.5 values on days with non-smoke plume overhead (HMS=0). This quantile derivation is designed to be fully consistent with the scope of the PM2.5-criteria calculation; specifically, for the GAM v2 dataset, Quant_PM2.5 is calculated according to each site's distinct monthly ECDF to account for seasonal shifts in the background distribution. The higher this value, the greater the statistical confidence that smoke has influenced the PM2.5 data. During significant smoke events, we typically observe many adjacent sites with high Quant_PM2.5 values (e.g., >95th percentile).

O3 due to smoke

The contribution of smoke to surface O3 is complex. On average, smoke increases surface O3 concentrations, but there is a large degree of variability due to varying emissions, meteorology during transport, smoke impacts on chemistry and photolysis and the local photochemical environment at the receptor site. Smoke can contribute to urban O3 concentrations either directly (e.g., O3 in the smoke plume mixes into an urban area) or in-directly, by enhancing photochemical production. For the in-direct enhancement, it is possible that VOCs in smoke plumes combine with existing urban NOx to enhance O3 production. It is also possible that PAN (peroxyacetyl nitrate) present in the smoke plume decomposes at the surface (due to warmer temperatures) and this will also contribute to O3 production. For all of these reasons, the contribution of smoke to surface O3 is highly variable and challenging to estimate (Jaffe et al., 2020).

To estimate the smoke contribution to surface O3 we use a statistical/machine learning approach to predict O3 in the absence of smoke. This is done using a "Generalized Additive Model" or GAM. For each site, the GAM estimates the MDA8 concentration in the absence of smoke using a variety of predictors. Importantly, the GAMs can include linear, non-linear and categorical relationships, so this means we can include typical met variables (such as daily max temperature, RH, etc.), plus variables such as the trajectory wind vector and speed, day of week, etc. as predictors for the MDA8. The GAMs must be calculated independently at each location since the relationship with the meteorological variables is different at each site. A variety of quality control measures are evaluated, as described in our various papers and publications.

Figure 2, below, shows performance of the GAM v2 for 803 sites nationally for data from April–October 2019–2024. This plot includes all observed and predicted MDA8 values and has a mean R2 of 0.83. The mean R2 for the individual GAMs at these 803 sites is 0.75. We define the model residuals as observed MDA8 – GAM predicted MDA8. On smoke days, we define the smoke O3 (SMO) to be equal to the residual.

Figure 3 shows the distribution of GAM residuals for no smoke and smoke days. The mean residual is 0 (SD of 4.9 ppb) on non-smoke days and 3.8 ppb (SD of 7.8 ppb) on smoke days, which is indicative of the mean smoke contribution to the MDA8. We use the 97.5th percentile of the non-smoke residuals to evaluate whether smoke has made a significant contribution to the MDA8, based on published guidance from the US EPA (US EPA, 2016). In this case, the residual for an individual smoke day must be greater than the 97.5th percentile of the non-smoke residuals. If it is, then we can consider the MDA8 to have been significantly enhanced by smoke. The 97.5th percentile of the non-smoke residuals is calculated individually at each site. The overall mean 97.5th percentile at all sites is 9.8 ppb, with a range of 5.4–16.0 ppb. This procedure is shown in Figure 3.

Similar to the PM2.5 discussion above, one variable to pay attention to in our datasets is the "Quant_MDA8O3_resids". This gives the percentile of each MDA8 residual, in comparison to the training (no smoke) data. The greater is this value, the greater is the confidence that smoke has influenced the O3 MDA8.

Empirical Distribution Matching (EDM) for bias correction in GAM

Statistical and/or machine learning regression models are inherently biased because the model is fitted closer to the center of the response variable. This problem is usually not rigorously considered in model evaluations. However, bias correction can be very important when considering how well any model fits observational data.

Belitz and Stackelberg (2021) proposed an Empirical Distribution Matching (EDM) method to correct the bias of regression models. EDM corrects the bias by aligning the empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) of observed and predicted values as follows:

YEDM = QTrObs ( FTrpred ( Ypred ) )

Where "F" is the ECDF, "Q" is the inverse ECDF, and "Tr" represents the training data (i.e., non-smoke days data in this study). For each site, QTrobs is obtained using observed MDA8, while FTrpred is calculated using GAM predicted MDA8. Then, GAM predicted MDA8 (Ypred) for both non-smoke and smoke days is input into these functions, Q and F, to finally obtain YEDM. Hereafter, this method will be referred to as GAM-EDM.

Figure 4 shows performance of the GAM v2-EDM for 803 sites nationally for data from April–October 2019–2024. The slope was slightly lower compared to Figure 2, and this is a natural phenomenon when applying bias correction. However, what is important is when we investigate true positives (TP) that exceed 70 ppb for both observed and predicted MDA8. The TP of GAM-EDM for training data (63%) improved by 18% compared to GAM (45%). As GAM-EDM adjusts predicted MDA8, the number of exceedance days with significant smoke contribution to MDA8 is slightly reduced. Being more conservative, GAM-EDM provides a more cautious estimate of exceedance days with significant smoke contribution to MDA8.
